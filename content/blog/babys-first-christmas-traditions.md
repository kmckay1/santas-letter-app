Baby's first Christmas tends to arrive before you're ready for it. You're still in the thick of early parenthood, not sleeping enough, and suddenly it's November and everyone wants to know what the baby wants for Christmas. (The baby wants to chew on the wrapping paper. That's the whole list.)

The gifts matter less than you think at this stage. What matters is which traditions you decide to start. Because some of them you'll still be doing when your kid is twelve.

Here are four worth starting in year one, selected not for how impressive they look in the moment but for how much they mean ten years from now.

## The Annual Handprint Ornament

This one feels small until you're pulling out a box of ornaments and the first one you find is a tiny clay disc with an impossibly small handprint pressed into it. Your child, eight months old. Then another from the following year, noticeably bigger. Then another.

The ritual is simple: in late November, press your child's hand into an air-dry clay disc (or use a kit), bake or let dry, paint it, write the year on the back. Hang it. Repeat every Christmas until they go to college and you dissolve a little inside.

The materials cost a few dollars. The object created costs nothing to maintain. And because you're doing it at the same time every year, it becomes something your child grows into rather than a one-time gesture. By age four, they're the ones who want to do it.

## The Christmas Eve Box

The Christmas Eve box is a relatively recent tradition, but it earns its place because it gives the night before Christmas its own ritual weight, separate from the next morning's gift chaos.

The contents can be whatever fits your family. The version that tends to stick: a new pair of Christmas pajamas, one Christmas book you read together before bed, and some small token tied to Santa's visit (a note, some reindeer food to scatter on the lawn, a single cookie left out ceremonially). The box itself gets reused each year. The pajamas get saved.

For babies and toddlers, the box is mostly for you. By age three, they start expecting it. By age five, they're asking about it in October.

## The Annual Photo Ritual

Every parent takes roughly five thousand photos in the first year of their child's life. The ones that matter ten years later are the intentional, repeated ones.

Pick one consistent Christmas photo setup and take it every year in the same spot: in front of the tree, same time of day, same general framing. The first year the baby won't cooperate. The second year the toddler will run away. By year three you're starting to have something. By year eight you have an eight-frame portrait of a child growing up in the same beloved house, measured against the same tree.

You don't need a good camera for this. You need consistency.

## The Letter from Santa

This is the tradition I'd argue is worth the most thought, because it's the one that diverges most sharply from a single-moment gift into something that compounds every year.

Most parents, when they think about getting a letter from Santa, think of it as a one-time gesture. An ornament for the occasion. But consider the alternative: a letter that starts the year your child is born and continues through age eight or nine, when the magic starts to fade.

What you'd have at the end isn't a letter. It's a record. Year one, Santa writes to a baby who doesn't know his own name yet, but whose parents have told Santa exactly who he already is: how his eyes went wide the first time he saw lights on a tree, how he belly-laughed at the dog, how his arrival changed everything. The letter is written to the child, but it's also written for the parents, in the way that a photograph taken today is for the person who looks back at it later.

Year two, Santa knows a little more. Year four, a lot more. By year seven, the letter knows this specific child in the way only a record of years can know anyone.

## Start small. Start now.

You don't need all four of these. Pick one or two that sound like you and actually do them. A tradition you start this year and sustain for a decade is worth more than five you start and abandon. The handprint and the photo are the lowest-lift. They require almost nothing and accumulate on their own.

The letter requires a little more intention, but it rewards that intention proportionally. A single letter from Santa is a gift your child will remember for a few years. A letter every Christmas from year one to year eight is something they might read to their own children someday.
