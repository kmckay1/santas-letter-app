## What makes a Santa letter work (and what makes one fall flat)

Before the structure or the wording, there's one principle worth stating plainly: a Santa letter earns its magic through specificity.

"I know you've been a very good child this year" is the sentence on every generic letter, in every Christmas movie, from every mail-order Santa service that doesn't ask you any questions. Kids know this. Even a five-year-old who absolutely believes in Santa knows that Santa letters are a thing people send. The moment a letter could have been written for any child, something deflates.

The letters that get saved, reread, and eventually framed are the ones where Santa says *your kid's name* and then says something only someone who'd been watching would know. "I heard about the afternoon you helped your little brother with his shoes" is worth more than three paragraphs of generic Christmas spirit.

This guide is built around that principle.

## The structure of a good Santa letter

A Santa letter has six parts. Not all of them are required, but each one serves a purpose.

**1. The salutation.** "Dear [name]" is fine. "My dear [name]" is warmer. Don't overthink it.

**2. A personal observation.** This is the most important sentence in the letter. Santa opens by naming something specific he noticed. An act of kindness, a challenge the child faced, a moment of growth. One specific thing, stated plainly, is worth more than five general compliments.

**3. Acknowledgment of the year.** A few sentences about who the child is and what they've worked on. School, a friendship, a sport, a creative pursuit. Keep it observational, not evaluative. "I heard you've been practicing piano every afternoon" lands better than "You've been such a hard worker."

**4. The wish response.** Santa acknowledges what the child asked for. This is the part children most anticipate. You don't need to commit to anything. "Your list arrived safely, and I've been giving it careful thought" is honest and delightful.

**5. A personal note or encouragement.** A sentence or two about something the child is navigating: starting a new school, being kind to a sibling, adjusting to a new baby, missing a grandparent. Santa sees everything. This is where the letter becomes a document of the year.

**6. The close.** Warm, brief. "With all the love and magic of Christmas, Santa Claus" is simple and correct. A P.S. is always welcome and often the part children remember best.

## What to include: the detail set

Gather these before you sit down to write. You won't use all of them, but having them in front of you means you can choose.

- Full name (the name the child actually goes by, not their formal name if different)
- Age
- One specific kind or brave thing they did this year (an act of generosity, a moment of courage, a time they put someone else first)
- One thing they've been working on (a skill, a subject, an activity)
- The wish list (what they actually asked for — one to three items is plenty)
- Something they're navigating or adjusting to (a move, a school transition, a new sibling)
- A named pet, if relevant
- A named friend or teacher, if relevant

Two or three of these details, used well, will carry a 300-word letter. More than five and it starts to feel like a résumé.

## Common wording mistakes

**Being too general in the observation section.** "You've been so kind this year" is not an observation. "I heard about the afternoon you found a classmate sitting alone at lunch and went to sit with her" is an observation. If you can't name something specific, ask yourself what you've actually seen your child do this year that you're proud of. Then write that.

**Listing too many accomplishments.** Three specific details outperform twelve generic ones. A letter that mentions soccer, piano, reading, kindness, a pet, a friendship, a trip, and a new school feels like a report card. Pick the two or three things that matter most and give them space.

**Over-explaining Santa's knowledge.** "I have been watching you through my magical telescope" is unnecessary. Santa simply knows. You don't need to explain the mechanism. The less you explain it, the more plausible it feels.

**Making the wish response too definitive.** Don't write "I'm bringing you exactly what you asked for." Santa doesn't promise. "I've given your list careful thought" or "your wishes arrived safely" is warmer and leaves appropriate room for Christmas morning.

**A generic close.** "Have a very merry Christmas" is not a close. It's a Hallmark card. Use the child's name. Reference something from earlier in the letter. "Have a wonderful Christmas, Lily, and say hello to Pepper for me" costs nothing and lands completely differently.

## A note on what Santa doesn't describe

Santa knows what children do. He does not describe what their house looks like, what they were wearing, what their room looks like, or anything else that the child might know their parents could see and you couldn't. Stay inside what Santa observes: actions, qualities, choices, feelings. That is the circle of things Santa notices, and staying inside it is what keeps the letter feeling real rather than surveilled.

## The actual writing

Set aside 30 to 45 minutes. Put your detail set in front of you. Read one of the examples above once more. Then write your version of each of the six sections. You do not need to write them in order. Most parents find the personal observation easiest to write last, once the rest of the letter is drafted and they can feel what tone they're aiming for.

Read it aloud when you're done. If it sounds like you talking, revise. If it sounds like a letter your child would believe arrived from someone who loves them and has been paying attention, you're done.

## Frequently asked questions

### How long should a Santa letter be?

Three hundred words is plenty for most children. Younger kids (four to six) do best with shorter letters. Older kids who are beginning to question Santa's existence often respond better to a letter that takes them seriously, which can run a little longer. There is no minimum. A short letter that is specific and warm outperforms a long letter that is generic.

### What details does Santa actually know?

Santa knows what children do: how they treat other people, how hard they work, what they care about, what challenges they faced. He does not describe physical details (what a child looks like, what their bedroom looks like) or anything their parents couldn't plausibly know. If a detail would make a child think "how would Santa know that?" in a suspicious way rather than a delighted way, leave it out.

### Should I mention what Santa is bringing?

Most parents don't. "Your list arrived safely and I've been giving it careful thought" is the standard move. If you want to confirm one item, you can. "The elves have a particular project underway" is enough of a hint for a child who is hoping for something specific. Don't commit to anything you're not certain is under the tree.

### How do I handle a child who is starting to doubt?

Write the letter as if they still believe, but calibrate the tone slightly older. Drop the younger whimsy (P.S. from Blitzen, workshop jokes) and lean into the parts that work regardless: genuine acknowledgment of who they are, what they've done, what they've been through this year. A child who is on the edge of not believing can still be moved by a letter that sees them clearly. That is the part of Santa that is real regardless of what they decide. For the research on where your child likely sits developmentally, see [what age kids stop believing in Santa](/blog/what-age-do-kids-stop-believing-in-santa).

### Is it okay to address something hard in the letter?

Yes, and it is often the right call. A move, a loss, a medical year, a friendship that ended, a school that was hard. Santa's acknowledgment of a hard thing ("I know this year has asked a lot of you") is one of the things that makes a letter worth keeping. Just keep it brief and forward-looking. Acknowledge it, hold it for a sentence or two, then move toward Christmas. You are writing toward hope, not into grief.
