There is a moment that only happens in houses with more than one child. Two letters get opened, and then almost immediately, the letters get swapped. Not out of suspicion. Out of ordinary curiosity about what Santa said to the other one.

That swap is the whole problem, and the whole opportunity.

An only child reads a letter from Santa in isolation. A slightly generic line goes unnoticed because there is nothing to compare it against. Siblings run an instant side-by-side comparison, and generic writing is exposed in seconds. If both letters praise being helpful this year and both mention being excited for Christmas morning, the shared language is obvious to a seven-year-old holding both pages.

The good news is that the same dynamic works in your favor when you get it right. Two genuinely different letters are more convincing to siblings than one letter is to an only child, because the difference itself becomes evidence. Santa clearly knows them separately.

## What has to be different

The instinct is to vary the gift mentions and leave the rest. That is not enough, because gifts are the part children expect to differ. The shared scaffolding is what gives it away.

**The specific detail.** This is the load-bearing element. Each letter needs at least one thing that belongs to that child and could not be moved to the other letter without becoming false. The thing they worked at this year. The thing they were nervous about. The interest that took over their spring. If you can swap a sentence between the two letters without editing it, it is not doing any work.

**The tone, matched to age.** A five-year-old and a nine-year-old should not receive letters that sound the same, and the gap in [what age kids stop believing](https://www.santasletter.ai/blog/what-age-do-kids-stop-believing-in-santa) is usually wider inside one household than parents assume. The younger letter can be warmer, simpler, more direct, with shorter sentences. The older letter can be slightly more conversational and can acknowledge that this child is growing up, which older siblings notice and quietly appreciate. Writing both at the same register makes the older one feel talked down to and the younger one feel lost.

**The length.** Related, and easy to overlook. A long letter to a four-year-old is a letter they will not finish. A short letter to a ten-year-old, sitting next to a longer one addressed to their little brother, reads as less effort. Length should track roughly with reading ability, and the gap should not be dramatic.

**What Santa asks of them.** A small, forward-looking line works well and is easy to differentiate. One child might be encouraged to keep going with something they have been practicing. Another might be encouraged in something entirely different. This is the part children reread.

## What should stay identical

Not everything should vary, and this is where people overcorrect.

The paper, the envelope style, the handwriting or typeface, the signature, and the general format should be the same across siblings. These are the markers of a common source. If one letter arrives on cream stationery with a wax seal and the other is printed on plain paper, the difference does not read as personal attention. It reads as one child mattering more.

The greeting and sign-off can be near identical. Nobody has ever been suspicious of two letters both ending warmly.

And the overall warmth has to be even. This is the one thing worth reading twice before sending. It is very easy to write a more affectionate letter to the child who had the more eventful year, or the one whose details came to you more easily. Read both letters back to back and ask whether one child could feel that Santa likes the other one more. That is the only fairness test that matters here, and it is not about word count.

## The fairness trap

Parents of siblings know the reflex: whatever one child gets, the other gets the equivalent. It is a reasonable instinct for presents and a poor one for letters.

Equal is not the same as identical. Two children who receive word-for-word parallel letters have not been treated equally, they have been treated as interchangeable. The thing a child actually wants from a letter is evidence of being known as a separate person, which is precisely what strict parallelism destroys.

The practical version: give each letter the same care, the same format, and the same affection. Let the content diverge as much as the children do.

## When the age gap is wide

The hardest version of this is a household where one child is deep in belief and another is on the way out. A six-year-old and an eleven-year-old are not in the same relationship with Santa, and pretending otherwise puts the older one in an awkward position.

A few approaches that work.

Write the older child's letter with a slightly different center of gravity. Less about whether they have been good, more about something they have become. Older children who are wobbling often stay in the story longer when the story treats them as a participant rather than an audience.

Consider whether the older child would rather be a keeper of the magic than a recipient of it. Many children around nine to eleven enjoy being quietly enlisted, and a letter can hint at that without ever stating it. If your older child has already worked it out, the [stopped believing](https://www.santasletter.ai/blog/letter-from-santa-stopped-believing) post covers how to handle that transition without ending Christmas for the younger ones.

If you are trying to judge where your older child actually is, the research on average ages is more reassuring than most parents expect, and a sibling household often extends belief rather than shortening it.

## Twins, and children close in age

Twins are the extreme case of the comparison problem, because the ages match exactly and there is no developmental difference to lean on. Everything has to come from personality.

This is where the detail gathering matters most. Before writing or ordering, write down three things for each twin that belong only to them. Not "they both love dinosaurs." What each one does with dinosaurs, or what each one got frustrated by, or what each one is quietly proud of. If you cannot come up with three separate things, that is worth noticing in itself, and ten minutes of thinking usually produces them.

The same applies to siblings fifteen months apart, who often get treated as a unit for practical reasons and feel it.

## The practical side of doing several

Writing two or three genuinely distinct letters by hand is real work, particularly in December. A few things make it manageable.

Gather the details for all the children first, in one sitting, before writing anything. Switching between gathering and writing is what makes this take an evening instead of twenty minutes. A short list per child is enough.

Write them on different days if you can. Writing three letters back to back is the main cause of accidental repetition, because the phrasing from the previous letter is still in your head.

Read them side by side at the end, in the order the children will open them. This catches both accidental repetition and accidental imbalance, and it takes two minutes.
