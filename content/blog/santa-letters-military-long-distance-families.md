There is a particular kind of Christmas that does not get written about much. A parent is deployed, or stationed overseas, or working a rotation that does not bend for December. The tree still goes up. The morning still happens. Somebody is missing from it.

This post is about planning a Santa letter around that situation. It is a different problem from ordering a gift from far away, because the thing you are working around is not distance in miles. It is absence on a specific day.

If you are a grandparent looking for gift ideas that land across a distance, the [long-distance grandparents](https://www.santasletter.ai/blog/long-distance-grandparents-christmas) post covers that situation directly. This one is for families where the distance sits inside the household.

## Why a physical letter does unusual work here

Most of the ways we bridge distance require both sides to be available at once. A video call needs a working connection, a compatible time zone, and a child who is in the mood. Deployments rarely cooperate with all three, and Christmas morning in one place is the middle of a shift in another.

A letter does not need anyone to be available. It arrives, it sits there, and the child opens it on their own schedule. That independence is the whole advantage. It is also why a physical letter tends to outperform a digital one in these households. A file on a screen competes with everything else on the screen. An envelope on the table does not.

There is a second thing worth naming. In a year when a child is being asked to accept a large absence, an object that arrived just for them, addressed to them, is a small counterweight. It is not a substitute for a parent. It is something concrete in a season that otherwise feels short by one person.

## Start with the mail dates, and work backward

This is the part that goes wrong most often, and it goes wrong early.

Military mail deadlines run substantially earlier than domestic ones, because the mail has further to travel and more handoffs to survive. The slowest and cheapest classes to APO, FPO, and DPO addresses have cut-offs in **November**, not December. Faster classes run into early and mid December. The exact dates move year to year, and USPS publishes the confirmed list on its holiday page each fall, so check **usps.com/holiday** for this year's dates rather than relying on last year's.

Two practical planning notes.

If the letter is going **to a child at an overseas or APO address**, treat the November deadlines as real and order or write in early November. A letter that misses the window does not arrive late, it arrives in January, which is a different thing entirely.

If the letter is going **to a child at home in the States** while a parent is deployed elsewhere, you have ordinary domestic timing, which is much more forgiving. This is the more common case and the easier one.

Either way, build in a buffer you would not normally need. The one year the mail runs slow is not the year to discover it.

## What the letter should actually say

This is the part people agonize over, and there is a reasonable answer.

The letter should acknowledge the situation, lightly, once. Not dwelling on it, not pretending it is not happening. A child who is carrying a parent's absence all season does not need a letter that talks around it, and they also do not need a letter that makes it the whole subject.

The version that works is usually a single sentence that recognizes this year is a different shape, followed by something forward-looking. What that sentence says depends entirely on what your family is actually doing, which is why nobody can write it for you.

Here is the rule worth holding to, and it matters more in this situation than any other. **Santa should only reference things a parent actually shared.** If nobody mentioned where the deployed parent is, Santa does not know where they are. If nobody described the house or the holiday plans, Santa has not seen them.

The temptation to add invented specifics is strongest here, because the emotional stakes are higher and invented detail feels like extra care. It is not. A child in a hard year is paying closer attention than usual, and a detail that does not match what they know creates a small wrongness at exactly the moment you were trying to reassure them. Give the letter real details and let them do the work.

One more thing that comes up often. Children in these families sometimes ask whether Santa knows where their mom or dad is. The workable answer is that Santa knows they are away and knows they are thinking about them, which is true in the only sense that matters and does not require you to invent a location.

## The mid-year move

Military families move, and a child who changed states in July is arriving at Christmas having lost their school, their friends, and their bedroom in the same year.

If that is your situation, the detail worth giving the letter is not the move itself. It is something the child did well inside it. The friend they made. The thing they tried at the new school. The way they handled a hard first month. A letter that recognizes the child coped with something, rather than one that mentions they moved, is the one that gets kept.

This also applies to the sibling dynamic, because a move usually hits an older child harder than a younger one, and their letters should reflect that difference rather than both mentioning the new house.

## Practical arrangements that help

A few things that families in this situation have found worth doing.

**Have the deployed parent supply the details.** Even if someone else places the order or writes the letter, the details can come from the parent who is away. It is a small act of participation in a Christmas they are otherwise absent from, and it usually produces better details, because the absent parent has been thinking about their child constantly.

**Decide who hands it over.** A letter that arrives in the mail and gets opened by the child is one experience. A letter that gets saved and given on Christmas Eve is another. Neither is better, but deciding in advance prevents the letter sitting in a pile of mail.

**Consider the digital copy as a backup, not the plan.** If the mail is uncertain, having a printable version means the child gets something on the day even if the envelope is delayed. The physical letter can then arrive whenever it arrives, which is a second event rather than a missed one.

**Do not wait for the deployment schedule to firm up.** It will not firm up. Order or write on the assumption that the situation stays as it is, because a letter that arrives when a parent unexpectedly made it home is a nice problem to have.
