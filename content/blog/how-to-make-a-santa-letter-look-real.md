A child evaluates a letter from Santa in about two seconds, and almost none of that assessment has to do with the words. They look at the paper. They look at the handwriting. They look at the envelope and whether it has the worn, traveled quality of something that came a long way.

If the object fails that first glance, the words have to work much harder. If the object passes, the words get the benefit of the doubt.

This is a guide to the physical side of a homemade Santa letter. It assumes you are writing it yourself and you want it to hold up. This post is about everything except the words.

## Start with the paper, because that is what gets touched

Standard printer paper is the single most common giveaway. It is bright white, it is thin, and every child in America handles it daily at school. It feels like a worksheet.

You want something with weight and a slightly warm tone. Look for a paper in the 28 to 32 pound range, or anything sold as resume or stationery paper. Cream, ivory, or natural white all read as older than bright white. A subtle laid or linen texture helps, because the surface catches the light differently from the flat paper children are used to.

You do not need to buy a ream. Most craft and office stores sell small packs, and a single sheet is all a letter requires.

Resist the urge to age it heavily. Tea staining and burned edges are the classic moves, and they are usually overdone. A letter that looks like a pirate treasure map does not look like mail. If you want a hint of age, brush a very weak tea solution over the sheet with a wide brush, let it dry completely flat under a heavy book overnight, and stop there. Uneven blotching looks like a craft project. A faint, even warmth looks like paper that has been somewhere.

## Handwriting is the part most people get wrong

Your child knows your handwriting better than you think. They have seen it on permission slips, lunch notes, and birthday cards for years. Writing the letter in your normal hand is the fastest way to lose them.

You have four reasonable options, in rough order of how convincing they are.

Ask someone else to write it. A grandparent, an aunt, a friend your child rarely gets mail from. This solves the problem completely, and it has the side benefit of producing handwriting that stays consistent year to year if the same person keeps doing it.

Write with your non-dominant hand. This changes your letterforms more than you would expect. It also slows you down, which produces the slightly labored quality of someone writing carefully. Practice a few lines first, because the first attempt usually looks like a ransom note.

Change the instrument. A fountain pen or a broad calligraphy nib forces different pressure and stroke width than the ballpoint you use daily. Combined with writing more slowly than usual, this shifts your hand noticeably.

Print in a script typeface. This is the least convincing option on its own, because printed text sits flat on the page in a way ink does not. If you go this route, sign the letter by hand in a contrasting ink, and consider adding a handwritten line in the margin. The mix of printed body and handwritten personal note reads better than either alone.

Whatever you choose, be consistent. If you plan to do this for several years, the handwriting should match the previous letters. Children go back and compare.

## Ink, and the case against black

Black ballpoint is the ink of homework and forms. Almost anything else reads better.

Brown, sepia, or a deep blue-black all look older and warmer. If you have access to a fountain pen, the natural variation in line width as the nib moves is genuinely hard to fake and does more for believability than any amount of paper aging.

Let the ink dry properly before folding. A smudge in the wrong place looks like a mistake. A very slight smudge in the right place, near the signature where a hand would naturally rest, looks like evidence of a real person writing.

## The envelope is doing more work than the letter

A crisp white business envelope undoes everything you did with the paper. Match the envelope to the sheet: same color family, some weight to it, ideally a slightly larger size than standard so it does not look like a bill.

Address it by hand, in the same ink and hand as the letter. Write your child's full name, including the middle name if they have one. Children are used to being addressed by first name only, and seeing their full name in ink on an envelope is a small, specific thrill.

For the return address, "Santa Claus, The North Pole" is the standard, and it is worth writing in the same hand rather than printing a label.

Then handle it a little. Real mail has been through machinery and sorting bins. An envelope with a soft crease at one corner and a slight bend looks traveled. An envelope that looks like it came out of a box looks like it came out of a box.

## The postmark, briefly

The United States Postal Service runs a free program that stamps your letter with a genuine North Pole postmark. In short, you write the letter yourself, put it in a stamped envelope addressed to your child with "SANTA, NORTH POLE" as the return address, then place that inside a larger envelope and mail the whole thing to the postmaster in Anchorage, Alaska, who postmarks it and sends it on.

It is genuinely free and genuinely magical, and it has a tight window in late November that is easy to miss. ## The wax seal, which is easier than it looks

A wax seal is the single highest-impact detail for the effort involved. Children almost never encounter sealing wax, which means it has no competing associations. It simply reads as old and official.

Basic kits with a brass stamp and wax sticks are inexpensive and widely available, and a snowflake, star, or letter S all work. Two practical notes. First, use a flexible wax rather than traditional brittle sealing wax, because a traditional seal will crack in the mail and arrive as crumbs. Flexible or "glue gun" sealing wax survives handling. Second, let the seal cool completely before the envelope goes anywhere. Warm wax will smear against the inside of the outer envelope and take the impression with it.

If a wax seal feels like too much, a simple sticker in gold or red, applied slightly off-center, does a reasonable amount of the same work.

## The imperfections that sell it

Perfect is the enemy here. Real handwritten letters have small flaws, and their absence is conspicuous.

A word that runs slightly downhill toward the end of a line. A letter that starts a little too close to the top edge. A comma that is darker because the pen paused. A fold that is not quite centered. None of these are mistakes, and a child cannot articulate why the letter feels real, only that it does.

The one thing to avoid is anything that makes the letter unreadable or fragile. Glitter is the classic offender. It looks wonderful for four seconds, gets everywhere, and makes the letter unpleasant to store in the memory box where you want it to end up in twenty years.

## The detail that outranks all of the above

You can get the paper, the ink, the seal, and the postmark exactly right, and the letter can still land flat. What actually convinces a child is not the artifact. It is being known.

A letter that references the specific thing your child was proud of this year, in their words, does more than any wax seal. A letter that mentions the thing they worried about, gently and without making it a lecture, is the one they keep.

This is also where homemade letters have a real advantage over anything bought, because you know the details and nobody else does. Use them. The one rule worth holding to is that Santa should only reference things your child actually told someone. Inventing a scene inside your house, or describing what your child looks like, tends to unsettle rather than delight, and it puts you in the position of maintaining a detail you made up.
